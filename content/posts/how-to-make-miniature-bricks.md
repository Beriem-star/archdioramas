Brick walls appear in almost every architectural diorama, and they are one of the first places where a model looks either convincing or toy-like. The good news: there are methods for every budget and skill level.

## Step 1: Get the brick size right

Start with a real brick size and divide it by your scale. A common standard brick measures **215 × 102.5 × 65 mm** (sizes vary by country, so use your local brick if you are modelling a specific place).

| Scale | Length | Depth | Height | Mortar joint (10 mm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1:12 | 17.9 mm | 8.5 mm | 5.4 mm | 0.8 mm |
| 1:20 | 10.8 mm | 5.1 mm | 3.3 mm | 0.5 mm |
| 1:24 | 9.0 mm | 4.3 mm | 2.7 mm | 0.4 mm |
| 1:35 | 6.1 mm | 2.9 mm | 1.9 mm | 0.3 mm |
| 1:50 | 4.3 mm | 2.1 mm | 1.3 mm | 0.2 mm |

Notice how thin the mortar becomes. Over-wide joints are the most common mistake in miniature brickwork. Not sure which scale to use? See [diorama scales explained](/blog/diorama-scales-explained/).

## Step 2: Choose a brick bond

The pattern bricks are laid in is called the **bond**. Getting it right adds instant realism:

- **Stretcher (running) bond** – every brick shows its long side, and each row is offset by half a brick. The most common modern pattern.
- **English bond** – alternating rows of long sides (stretchers) and short ends (headers). Typical of older, thicker walls.
- **Flemish bond** – stretchers and headers alternate within the same row. Common on historic façades.

## Method 1: Engraved foam (easiest)

1. Cut a sheet of **XPS foam** (extruded polystyrene; the dense, smooth insulation board) to wall size.
2. Draw the horizontal mortar lines with a ruler and a blunt pencil or empty ballpoint pen.
3. Draw the vertical joints, offsetting every other row.
4. Press a crumpled ball of foil over the surface for texture, and poke a few small pits.
5. Seal with a coat of diluted PVA or acrylic before painting (solvent-based spray paint melts foam).

**Best for:** large walls, ruins, quick projects.

## Method 2: Printed brick texture

Print a scaled brick texture on matte paper and glue it to card or MDF. Add depth by lightly scoring the mortar lines with a blunt tool. This is ideal for background buildings and small scales such as 1:87, where engraving is hard.

**Best for:** background buildings, small scales, beginners.

## Method 3: Laser-engraved MDF or wood

A laser cutter can engrave the mortar lines directly into 2–3 mm MDF or plywood while cutting the wall outline, door and windows in the same job. The result is perfectly regular, so add variation when painting. See our comparison of [laser-cut vs 3D-printed parts](/blog/laser-cut-vs-3d-printed-diorama-parts/).

**Best for:** architectural models, repeated wall modules, crisp detail.

## Method 4: 3D-printed brick sheets

Print thin brick panels (1–2 mm) or modular wall sections. Print them flat for the sharpest top surface, and use a small layer height to reduce visible layer lines. Modular, stackable wall pieces let you build corners, ruins and partial walls without redesigning anything.

**Best for:** modular walls, ruins, corners and curved shapes.

## Method 5: Individual clay bricks (most realistic)

For the most authentic look, especially at 1:20 or larger, make real bricks one by one:

1. Press clay into a small silicone or 3D-printed mould sized for your scale.
2. Let the bricks dry slowly and evenly to avoid cracks.
3. Fire them (real clay) or simply let them cure (air-dry clay).
4. Lay them on a wall core with a mortar of fine filler or tile grout, exactly like a real wall.

This is slow, but the colour variation and texture are impossible to fake. Air-dry clay needs no kiln, but is more fragile than fired clay.

**Best for:** close-up 1:12 to 1:20 builds, construction-site scenes, showpieces.

## Painting realistic brick

1. **Base coat** the whole wall in a light grey or beige mortar colour.
2. **Paint the brick faces** with a sponge or flat brush so the colour stays off the joints. Use two or three slightly different reds and browns, picking random bricks for each.
3. **Mortar wash**: brush on thinned filler or very diluted light paint, then wipe the surface with a damp sponge so it stays only in the joints.
4. **Weather**: a dark wash at the bottom of the wall, a few streaks below windows, and a light dry-brush over the brick faces.

## Which method should you choose?

| Method | Cost | Speed | Realism | Tools needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engraved foam | Very low | Fast | Good | Pencil, ruler |
| Printed texture | Very low | Very fast | Fair to good | Printer |
| Laser-engraved MDF | Low | Fast | Very good | Laser cutter |
| 3D-printed sheets | Low | Medium | Very good | 3D printer |
| Individual clay bricks | Low | Slow | Excellent | Mould, clay, optional kiln |

Start with engraved foam or a printed texture on your first diorama, then move to engraved MDF or real clay bricks once you want close-up realism.

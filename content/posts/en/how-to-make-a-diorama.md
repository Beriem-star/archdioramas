A diorama is a small, three-dimensional scene: a corner shop at night, a ruined wall overgrown with plants, a tiny workshop full of tools. Unlike a single model, a diorama shows **a place and a moment**, so it needs ground, context and a little story.

This guide walks you through a complete first build, in the same order we use for the projects on our channel.

## Step 1: Choose an idea and a scale

Start with one sentence that describes the scene, for example: *"A small brick shop front on a quiet street, just after rain."* That sentence decides what you need to build and, just as important, what you can leave out.

Next, pick a scale. If you are unsure, read our guide to [diorama scales](/blog/diorama-scales-explained/). For a first project, **1:24** or **1:20** is a comfortable size: large enough to handle, small enough to finish.

## Step 2: Gather tools and materials

You do not need a workshop. A beginner kit looks like this:

| Item | What it is for |
|---|---|
| Cutting mat, steel ruler, hobby knife with spare blades | Accurate, safe cuts |
| MDF, plywood or foam board (5–10 mm) | A rigid base that will not warp |
| Card, foam board or 2–3 mm MDF | Walls, floors and roofs |
| PVA or wood glue, plus a little superglue | Joining parts |
| Filler or lightweight spackle | Ground, plaster and stone textures |
| Acrylic paints and a few brushes | Base colours and weathering |
| Sandpaper (120–400 grit) | Cleaning edges and joints |

Change blades often. A sharp blade is safer than a blunt one because you press less.

## Step 3: Plan the layout

Sketch the scene from above and from the front. Decide where the viewer will stand and make sure the most interesting part faces them. Useful rules:

- **Avoid placing everything parallel to the edges.** Turning a building or street a few degrees makes the scene feel natural.
- **Leave breathing room.** A little empty ground around the main subject makes it stand out.
- **Think in layers:** ground, structures, details, life (figures, plants, light).

If you are using one of our plans, print the template at 100% and check the scale bar with a ruler before cutting.

## Step 4: Build the base

Cut the base to size and seal it with a coat of primer or diluted PVA so it does not absorb moisture and warp. Mark the footprint of buildings, pavements and roads in pencil.

For ground texture, spread a thin layer of filler or spackle and dab it with an old brush. For a pavement or road, press lines into the filler while it is still soft, or glue on thin card cut into slabs.

## Step 5: Build the structures

Cut walls following the plan, then:

1. **Test-fit before gluing.** Dry-assemble the walls with tape to check corners and openings.
2. **Glue walls to a floor plate**, not directly to the base. This keeps everything square.
3. **Add wall thickness at openings.** Window and door reveals are where viewers notice whether a model looks real.
4. **Add texture before assembly when you can.** Engraving bricks or stone on a flat wall is much easier than on a finished box.

For brick walls, see our guide on [how to make miniature bricks](/blog/how-to-make-miniature-bricks/).

## Step 6: Add details

Details tell the story. A few well-chosen items beat dozens of random ones:

- Door handles, light fittings, signs and gutters
- Crates, buckets, tools, bicycles
- Plants: lichen, foam flock, dried roots or 3D-printed pots
- Wires and pipes made from thin wire or stretched plastic

Ask of every detail: *does this help tell my one-sentence story?* If not, leave it out.

## Step 7: Paint

1. **Prime everything** in a neutral grey, black or brown so paint sticks evenly.
2. **Block in base colours** with thin coats.
3. **Use a wash** (very diluted dark paint) to fill recesses, mortar lines and corners.
4. **Dry-brush** a lighter colour over raised textures to make them pop.

Real colours look too strong at small sizes. Mix a little grey or beige into your paints to soften them; this is often called *scale colour*.

## Step 8: Weather the scene

Weathering is what makes a diorama believable. Keep it subtle and logical:

- Dirt collects at the bottom of walls and in corners.
- Rain streaks run down from windowsills and roof edges.
- Paint fades and chips where people touch or walk.

Build up weathering in thin layers. It is easy to add more, and hard to remove.

## Step 9: Add lighting (optional)

Warm white LEDs inside a building instantly add life, especially in night scenes. Plan cable routes before gluing the roof, drill a hole through the base for the wires, and use a small battery box or USB power. Block light leaks at wall joints with black paint or tape on the inside.

## Step 10: Final touches and display

Add figures last so they do not get damaged. Glue a thin edge trim around the base, add a small label with the title and scale, and protect the scene from dust with a clear cover if you plan to keep it.

## Your first project checklist

- One-sentence story written
- Scale chosen and written on the plan
- Base cut and sealed
- Walls test-fitted, then glued
- Details chosen to support the story
- Primed, painted, washed, dry-brushed
- Weathered in thin layers
- Figures and labels added last

Diorama Scales Explained: 1:12, 1:20, 1:24, 1:35, 1:50, 1:87 and More

What diorama and model scales mean, how big a person, door or brick is at each scale, and how to choose the right scale for your first build.

By ArchDioramas · Updated November 22, 2026 · 4 min read

KEY TAKEAWAYS

  • A scale like 1:50 means 1 unit on the model equals 50 of the same units in real life.
  • To convert, divide the real size by the scale number: a 1.75 m person is 35 mm tall at 1:50.
  • 1:12 and 1:24 are dollhouse scales, 1:35 is the classic military diorama scale, 1:50 and 1:100 are common for architectural models, and 1:87 (HO) is model-railway scale.
  • Pick the scale first, then buy figures, furniture and materials that match it, so everything looks believable together.

Every diorama starts with one decision that affects everything else: the scale. Choose it first and your walls, doors, furniture and figures will all look like they belong in the same world. Mix scales by accident and the scene will look "off" even if every part is well made.

This guide explains what scale numbers mean, gives you a ready-made size chart, and helps you pick a scale for your next build.

What does a scale like 1:50 mean?

A scale is a ratio. 1:50 means that 1 millimetre on your model represents 50 millimetres in real life. The same works with any unit: 1 cm on the model is 50 cm in reality.

The key rule: the bigger the number after the colon, the smaller the model. A 1:12 dollhouse chair is large enough to hold in your palm; a 1:160 chair is smaller than a grain of rice.

How to convert real sizes to model sizes

Use one simple formula:

Model size = real size ÷ scale number

Examples:

  • A door 2,100 mm high at 1:20 → 2,100 ÷ 20 = 105 mm
  • A room 3,000 mm wide at 1:50 → 3,000 ÷ 50 = 60 mm
  • A person 1,750 mm tall at 1:35 → 1,750 ÷ 35 = 50 mm

To go the other way (from a model measurement back to real life), multiply by the scale number.

How to choose the right scale for your diorama

Ask yourself four questions:

1. How much space do you have?

A small house at 1:12 can easily be 60 cm wide. The same house at 1:50 fits in the palm of your hand. Measure the shelf or display case first and work backwards.

2. How much detail do you want?

Larger scales (1:12 to 1:24) reward fine detail: individual bricks, door handles, tiny books. Smaller scales (1:87 and below) are better for whole streets, landscapes and buildings seen from a distance.

3. What parts can you buy or download?

Figures, furniture, plants and vehicles are sold in specific scales. If you want to add ready-made people, choose a scale where they are easy to find, such as 1:35, 1:87 or 1:50.

4. Which tools will you use?

Laser cutters and 3D printers both have practical limits. Very thin parts (under about 0.8 mm) can warp, burn or snap. If a part becomes too fragile at a small scale, step up to a larger one.

Common scale mistakes to avoid

  • Mixing "almost the same" scales. 1:48 and 1:50 look close on paper, but a 1:48 figure next to a 1:50 doorway is noticeably taller. Stick to one scale per scene.
  • Using real-world material thickness. A 3 mm sheet of MDF represents a 15 cm thick wall at 1:50, but a 6 cm thick slab at 1:20. Always check that thicknesses make sense at your scale.
  • Forgetting the base. Plan the base size in scale too, so pavements, kerbs and steps are believable.
  • Scaling texture too big. Wood grain, bricks and tiles must shrink along with everything else. Oversized textures are one of the first things that make a model look like a toy.

Quick reference: scale and material thickness

Here is what common sheet thicknesses represent in real life:

Sheet thickness At 1:20 At 1:35 At 1:50
1 mm 2 cm 3.5 cm 5 cm
2 mm 4 cm 7 cm 10 cm
3 mm 6 cm 10.5 cm 15 cm

A typical interior wall is around 10–15 cm thick, so at 1:50 a 2–3 mm sheet works well, while at 1:20 you would laminate two or three layers.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common scale for dioramas?

It depends on the subject. Military dioramas are usually 1:35, model railway scenes 1:87 (HO), dollhouses 1:12, and architectural models 1:50 or 1:100. There is no single standard for every hobby.

How do I convert real measurements to a model scale?

Divide the real measurement by the scale number. A 2.1 m door at 1:20 is 2100 mm ÷ 20 = 105 mm tall. To go back from model to real size, multiply by the scale number.

Is 1:24 bigger than 1:50?

Yes. The smaller the number after the colon, the bigger the model. A 1:24 model is roughly twice the size of the same object at 1:50.

What scale is best for a beginner diorama?

A mid scale such as 1:24 or 1:35 is forgiving: parts are big enough to handle and detail, but the finished scene still fits on a shelf. 1:20 is also a good choice if you want to build realistic walls and bricks.
